Walk down almost any street in the Old North or the City Centre and you'll see it: scaffolding wrapped around a 1960s block, two new floors going up on the roof, a lift shaft bolted to the side. That's urban renewal, and for twenty years most of it in Tel Aviv happened under a national plan called TAMA 38. Here's what it is, what's changed, and what it means for you whether you live in the building, next door, or are about to sign a lease.

## What TAMA 38 is

TAMA 38 (תמ״א 38) was a national plan launched in 2005 to strengthen older buildings against earthquakes. Instead of the state paying, it let developers add new apartments to a building in return for doing the strengthening work for free. The owners got a reinforced building, usually with a new safe room (mamad), a lift, a renovated entrance and sometimes parking; the developer got new apartments to sell.

It came in two versions:

- **TAMA 38/1, strengthening and adding.** The building stays. It's reinforced, and the developer adds one to three floors on top and sometimes extends the existing flats. Residents usually stay in their homes during the works.
- **TAMA 38/2, demolish and rebuild.** The old building is knocked down and a bigger one built in its place. Owners move out for the duration, typically two to three years, with the developer paying their rent, and get a new, larger apartment at the end.

You'll see both labelled "TAMA 38" in the city's permit records and on our [construction checker](/check).

## What changed: TAMA 38 has ended in Tel Aviv

The national plan was wound down in 2024. Cities were asked to replace it with their own local renewal plans, and those that met the conditions got an extension. Tel Aviv-Yafo did not file its replacement in time, so TAMA 38 stopped applying in the city at the end of August 2024. Projects that already had permits or were far enough along in the process continue, which is why you'll still see plenty of TAMA 38 work around the city.

Its replacement is a city-wide plan called **TA/5555** (תא/5555). The city announced it in August 2025 and moved it into the statutory approval process. It's meant to set building rights plot by plot, keep both routes (strengthening and adding, or demolish and rebuild), and shorten the path to a permit. Planning processes take time, so check the plan's current status with the city before relying on it for a specific building.

Alongside it, larger projects go through **pinui binui** (פינוי-בינוי, "evacuate and build"): whole blocks or groups of buildings demolished and replaced with towers under a detailed local plan.

## If you live in the building

Renewal can be the best thing that ever happens to an old building, or a long headache. A few things decide which:

- **Owners decide, not tenants.** Under the law, a project needs the agreement of a large majority of the owners. If you rent, your landlord's lease decides what happens to you. Ask before you sign whether the building has signed with a developer.
- **Get your own lawyer.** Owners are usually represented by a lawyer paid by the developer but chosen by the owners. Make sure that's the case, and that the agreement covers guarantees, timelines, the specification of the new flats, and rent during a demolish-and-rebuild.
- **Expect years, not months.** A strengthening-and-adding project typically takes 18 to 30 months on site; demolish and rebuild two to three years after the permit, and the planning stage before that can take years too.
- **Living through it.** In strengthening projects you stay put while scaffolding, drilling and concrete pours happen around you. Ask for the work schedule, the site manager's number and how deliveries will be handled.

## If you live next door

You don't get a vote, but you do get the noise. What to know:

- Building work is allowed from 07:00 to 19:00 on weekdays, and not on Shabbat or holidays. Night work needs a special approval. See our guide to [construction noise rules](/construction-noise-rules).
- The noisiest phases are demolition, digging and foundations (especially basements for parking), then the concrete frame. Finishing works are much quieter.
- Permit requests are public before they're approved. Look up your address on our [checker](/check) to see what's been filed around you, and [sign up for alerts](/check) to hear about new requests.

## If you're apartment hunting

Renewal is one of the biggest things that can change your life in a flat, so check before you sign:

1. **Is the building itself in a project?** Ask the landlord or seller directly. For a purchase, your lawyer should check the building's planning file.
2. **What's happening next door?** Type the address into our [construction checker](/check). It shows permit requests under review, approved permits and active sites within 100 to 500 metres, with a noise score for each.
3. **How long is left?** A site that started three years ago is probably near the end; a permit just approved means the loud part is still ahead.
4. **Compare options.** Put two or three addresses side by side with our [apartment comparison](/compare).

## FAQ

### Is TAMA 38 still available in Tel Aviv?

No new TAMA 38 applications are accepted in Tel Aviv since the end of August 2024. Projects already in the system continue, and the city's replacement plan, TA/5555, is going through the approval process.

### What's the difference between TAMA 38 and pinui binui?

TAMA 38 works building by building, either strengthening and adding floors or demolishing and rebuilding one building. Pinui binui replaces several buildings or a whole block at once under a detailed plan, usually with towers.

### How long does a TAMA 38 project take?

On site, roughly 18 to 30 months for strengthening and adding floors, and two to three years for demolish and rebuild. The planning and permit stage before that often takes several years.
