Tel Aviv has quietly become one of the great restaurant cities of the Mediterranean. The style is its own: loud, generous and informal, with chefs cooking in open kitchens, bartenders pouring arak over ice, and plates meant for sharing arriving whenever they're ready. Even the city's best restaurants rarely feel stuffy. The dress code is "nice jeans".

What ties the best kitchens together is the produce. Israel's markets overflow with tomatoes, herbs, citrus, fish from the Mediterranean and cheeses from small dairies, and chefs build menus around what's good that week. Expect lots of vegetables, olive oil and fire.

A few practical things: book ahead, especially for Thursday and Friday nights. Many restaurants have two seatings. Counter seats at the bar are often the most fun, and easier to get. And check whether the place is kosher; most of the restaurants below are not, and are open on Friday night and Saturday.

## The picks

[[pick: Taizu | A stylish, high-energy room on Menachem Begin Road and one of the most acclaimed restaurants in Israel. The menu draws on the street food of Asia, from India to China and Southeast Asia, reimagined as small and large plates to share. Great cocktails, too. Book well in advance.]]

[[pick: Claro | Chef Ran Shmueli's restaurant in a beautifully restored Templer building in Sarona. Mediterranean cooking built around a wood oven and local produce, in a room of old stone and high ceilings. Open from lunch until late, and a lovely choice for a celebration.]]

[[pick: Yaffo Tel Aviv | Chef Haim Cohen's restaurant, a pioneer of modern Israeli cooking that draws on the many food traditions that came together in this country. A grown-up, elegant evening.]]

[[pick: DOK | A tiny restaurant on Ibn Gvirol run by brothers who cook only with local, seasonal ingredients, many of them from small Israeli farms and producers. The menu changes constantly. Intimate and special: perfect for a date.]]

[[pick: HaAchim | "The Brothers", from the same team as DOK, is the louder, bigger sibling: a buzzing room serving generous Israeli dishes, grilled meats and vegetables to share. Come with friends, order lots and expect a great atmosphere.]]

[[pick: Abraxas | Eyal Shani's North Abraxas on Lilienblum Street, where the chef who reinvented the pita serves his signature dishes, like the whole roasted cauliflower and tomato salads, in a loud, joyful room. The music is up, the vibe is party, and the food is Tel Aviv on a plate.]]

[[pick: Herzl 16 | A relaxed bar-restaurant near Neve Tzedek, open from breakfast until midnight. Less of an occasion than the others but perfect for a laid-back date with good food and drinks.]]

[[pick: Kitchen Market | A restaurant above the farmers' market in a hangar at the Tel Aviv Port, with views over the sea and the Yarkon and a menu that follows the market's produce. Great at sunset.]]

[[pick: Pastel | The restaurant of the Tel Aviv Museum of Art: a chic, airy room for lunch or dinner, ideal to combine with a visit to the museum.]]

[[pick: Port Said | Not a fancy restaurant at all, but a perfect late-night date: the pavement bar next to the Great Synagogue, with records playing, simple plates and the city's creative crowd all around.]]

## FAQ

### What is the best restaurant in Tel Aviv?

There's no single answer, but Taizu, Claro, DOK and Eyal Shani's North Abraxas are regularly named among the city's best. For a special occasion, book one of them well ahead.

### Do I need to book restaurants in Tel Aviv?

For the popular places, yes, especially on Thursday and Friday nights. Many restaurants take online bookings. Bar seats are often kept for walk-ins.

### What time do people eat dinner in Tel Aviv?

Later than in northern Europe: 20:30 to 21:30 is typical, and restaurants stay busy until late.

### Are Tel Aviv restaurants open on Friday night and Saturday?

Most non-kosher restaurants are open through Shabbat. Kosher restaurants close from Friday afternoon until Saturday night. See [kosher restaurants](/magazine/kosher-restaurants-tel-aviv).

### What should I wear?

Smart casual is fine almost everywhere. Tel Aviv is relaxed: you'll rarely see jackets or ties.
