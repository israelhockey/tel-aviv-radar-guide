Finding a flat in Tel Aviv is a competitive sport. Good apartments go in days, sometimes hours, and the person who turns up with questions ready and a deposit arranged usually wins. This guide walks through the whole process, from the first search to the day you get the keys, with the checks that save you from a year of regret.

## Where apartments are listed

- **Yad2** is the biggest listings site. Set alerts for your streets, price and number of rooms, and check them several times a day.
- **Madlan** shows listings with neighbourhood data and price history, which helps you judge whether an asking price is fair.
- **Facebook groups** are where many of the best flats appear first, often posted by the outgoing tenant. Search for groups by neighbourhood ("דירות להשכרה בפלורנטין", "apartments for rent Old North") and turn on notifications.
- **Walking around** still works. Look for "להשכרה" (for rent) signs on balconies in the streets you like.

Facebook and Yad2 listings often hide the exact address. Ask for it before the viewing, then [check it on our map](/check) to see building permits, building sites and night works around it.

## Before you start: know your budget and your must-haves

Rooms in Israel are counted differently: a "3-room apartment" (3 חדרים) means two bedrooms plus a living room. Kitchens and bathrooms are not counted. A "2.5" usually means a small extra room that fits a desk or a cot.

On top of the rent, budget for:

- **Arnona** (city property tax), usually paid by the tenant. See our [arnona guide](/magazine/arnona-tel-aviv-explained).
- **Va'ad bayit** (building committee fee) for cleaning, lighting and the lift.
- **Electricity, water and gas**, billed separately.
- **Internet**, which you arrange yourself.

## What to check at a viewing

Bring this list and go through it every time. It takes ten minutes.

1. **Safe room or shelter.** Is there a mamad (reinforced room) in the flat, a shared shelter in the building, or a public shelter nearby? Check our [shelters and emergencies guide](/guide).
2. **Damp and mould.** Look in corners, behind wardrobes and under sinks. A fresh coat of paint in one spot can hide a leak.
3. **Water pressure and hot water.** Turn on the shower. Ask whether the boiler is solar (dud shemesh) with an electric backup, and where the switch is.
4. **Air conditioning.** How old are the units, and is there one in each bedroom?
5. **Windows and light.** Which way does the flat face? South- and west-facing flats get hot in summer.
6. **Noise.** Come back once in the evening. Bars, buses and deliveries change a street completely after dark.
7. **Construction.** Ask the landlord whether the building has a TAMA 38 or demolish-and-rebuild plan, and [look up the address](/check) for permits on the neighbouring plots. A building site next door can mean two to three years of drilling from 7am.
8. **Parking.** If you have a car, ask about a resident parking sticker and whether the building has spaces.
9. **Who lives there.** A quick chat with a neighbour on the stairs tells you more than any listing.

## The lease: what it must include

Israeli leases are usually for 12 months, often with an option to extend. Read it before you pay anything, and have someone who reads Hebrew go through it with you if you don't.

Make sure the lease states:

- the rent, the payment dates and how you pay (usually post-dated cheques or a standing order),
- the length of the lease and how either side can end it early,
- who pays arnona, va'ad bayit and utilities,
- the security you're giving and when it will be returned,
- a description of the flat and its condition, including the appliances that stay,
- whether you may sublet or find a replacement tenant if you need to leave early.

Take dated photos of every room on the day you move in and send them to the landlord. It's the simplest way to avoid arguments about the deposit later.

## Your rights under the Fair Rental Law

Since 2017, Israeli law sets minimum standards for most residential leases. In plain terms:

- **The apartment must be fit to live in** when you get the keys: a door that locks, running water, electricity, working drainage, and no defects that put your health or safety at risk.
- **Repairs are the landlord's job** for anything that isn't caused by you. Urgent problems that make the flat unfit to live in must be fixed within 3 days of you reporting them; other repairs within 30 days. Normal wear and tear is not your cost.
- **Security is capped.** The landlord can't ask for more than three months' rent or a third of the total rent for the lease, whichever is lower, and must return it within 60 days of the lease ending unless there's a genuine claim against it.
- **Some costs are always the landlord's:** building insurance, and building-wide repairs or improvements decided by the building committee. Regular va'ad bayit and arnona are usually the tenant's.
- **The lease must be in writing.**

The law covers most ordinary leases; very expensive rentals may fall outside it. This is a summary, not legal advice. If something goes wrong, organisations like Kol Zchut publish the full rules in Hebrew, English and other languages.

## Brokers

Many listings go through a broker (מתווך). A broker can only charge you if you signed a brokerage agreement with them, usually a short form at the viewing. Read it before you sign, and ask upfront what the fee is and who pays it. Typical fees are about one month's rent plus VAT.

## Security and guarantors

Landlords usually ask for a combination of: post-dated cheques for the rent, a security deposit or bank guarantee, and one or two guarantors (ערבים) who sign the lease with you. If you're new to Israel and don't have local guarantors, a bank guarantee or a larger deposit (within the legal cap) are the usual alternatives.

## Moving in

1. Register for arnona with the city in your name from the day the lease starts.
2. Transfer electricity, water and gas accounts to your name, and note the meter readings.
3. Update your address with the Population Authority if you're a resident.
4. [Sign up for construction alerts](/check) on your new address so you hear about new permits on your street before the scaffolding goes up.

## FAQ

### How far in advance should I start looking?

About a month before you want to move. Most flats are listed four to six weeks before they're free, and good ones go quickly, so be ready to decide on the spot.

### Can the landlord raise the rent during the lease?

Not unless the lease says so. Rent increases are usually negotiated when the lease is renewed. Some leases link rent to the consumer price index; check for this clause.

### Who pays for a broken boiler or air conditioner?

The landlord, unless you caused the damage. Report it in writing (WhatsApp is fine) and keep a record. Urgent faults must be fixed within 3 days, others within 30.

### How do I know if a building site will start next door?

Look up the address on our [construction checker](/check). It shows permit requests under review, approved permits, active building sites and night works around any Tel Aviv address, from the city's own records.
